Agricultural output and productivity growth have slowed across many of the world’s major growing regions as rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and more frequent extremes increasingly constrain production systems. 

While a substantial literature documents climate impacts on yields and productivity, far less is known about how agricultural production structures themselves adjust over time to climate variability. 

This study examines system-level adjustment over 1961-2021 in four major agricultural regions with substantial rain-fed exposure: North America, Central Europe, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Australia. 

Using cointegration analysis and vector error-correction models, we distinguish short-run climate-driven deviations from long-run equilibrium relationships linking temperature anomalies and water-availability conditions to agricultural productivity and key production components. 

Corrected estimates show statistically significant aggregate TFP equilibrium correction only in Sub-Saharan Africa, while Australia, North America, and Central Europe display weak or inconclusive TFP adjustment. 

Component-level adjustment is more evident but concentrated in selected margins, especially fertiliser use and some livestock and capital-labour systems. 

These results suggest that productivity adjustment alone should not be interpreted as evidence of broad agricultural resilience. 

Instead, climate-related adjustment appears margin- and region-specific, with flexible inputs responding more readily than slower-moving structural components. 

The findings connect agricultural production adjustment to food-security risks by highlighting potential constraints on food availability, food supply stability, price transmission, and access under intensifying climate stress.
